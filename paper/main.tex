\begin{abstract}
Teammate features in decentralized multi-agent reinforcement learning are often concatenated in an arbitrary fixed order, even when teammates are exchangeable. We study whether reducing this order sensitivity improves zero-shot coordination with unseen partners. Our testbed is \emph{PICR-MA}, a lightweight policy-side regularizer that samples two independent permutations of teammate feature blocks and penalizes the symmetric Kullback--Leibler divergence between the resulting action distributions. \method\ leaves the inference-time policy architecture unchanged and adds only extra training-time forward passes. We evaluate \method\ against IPPO, Deep Sets, and Other-Play on a 5-seed, 50k-step PettingZoo MPE cohort (\texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}) using same-family held-out partner cross-play, permutation sensitivity, and runtime diagnostics; matrix-game sanity checks are retained only as appendix support. We also add a second matched 5-seed IPPO-versus-PICR-MA replication cohort at the same MPE budget. Across both cohorts, \method\ consistently lowers permutation sensitivity. The coordination effect is much less stable: the positive \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} cross-play delta remains wide in both cohorts, and the original negative \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} delta reverses sign in the replication cohort. The main contribution of the paper is therefore not a new default MARL recipe, but a controlled empirical result: permutation-consistency diagnostics are easier to reproduce than held-out coordination gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Zero-shot coordination (ZSC) asks whether an agent trained in self-play can cooperate with an unfamiliar partner at test time \citep{hu2020otherplay}. In decentralized MARL, one source of ZSC brittleness is mundane but pervasive: teammate features are commonly fed to a multilayer perceptron in a fixed concatenation order, so the policy may learn distinct roles for the ``first'' and ``second'' teammate even when the environment is symmetric. This creates an avoidable dependence on an arbitrary observation convention.

This paper studies a minimal intervention at the policy level. Rather than changing the policy architecture to a set encoder or graph network \citep{zaheer2017deepsets, liu2020pic}, we regularize the policy to produce similar action distributions under different teammate-order views of the same observation. The resulting method, \method, is intentionally simple: during training we apply two independent permutations to teammate feature blocks and penalize their action-distribution disagreement with a symmetric KL loss.

The interesting question is not only whether this penalty reduces a permutation-sensitivity diagnostic. The more important question is whether that diagnostic reduction improves downstream coordination with held-out partners. The answer in the current artifact is less stable than the diagnostic change itself. Across two matched 5-seed IPPO-versus-\method\ cohorts, the permutation-sensitivity shift is reproducible, while the coordination effect remains wide on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} and even flips sign across cohorts on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}. This mismatch is the main scientific point of the workshop paper.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em]
\item We implement \method\ as a policy-side permutation-consistency regularizer for shared-parameter decentralized MARL with no inference-time architectural change.
\item We build a controlled evaluation stack with held-out partner cross-play, explicit teammate-block slicing, permutation-sensitivity diagnostics, runtime tracking, and a second matched IPPO-versus-\method\ replication cohort for the main MPE tasks.
\item We show that \method\ reliably reduces permutation sensitivity across both matched MPE cohorts, but its effect on held-out coordination is materially less stable: positive but wide on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, and sign-flipping across cohorts on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}.
\item We identify a concrete diagnostic failure mode for this family of ideas: lower teammate-order sensitivity does not by itself imply better zero-shot coordination.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
Each agent observation is decomposed into non-teammate features $s$ and teammate-specific blocks $U = [u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}]$. For two independently sampled teammate permutations $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, we form
\begin{align}
o_{(1)} &= [s \Vert \pi_1(U)], &
p_1 &= \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{(1)}), \\
o_{(2)} &= [s \Vert \pi_2(U)], &
p_2 &= \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{(2)}).
\end{align}
The regularizer is
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{\method}} =
\lambda \, \E \Big[
\tfrac{1}{2}\KL(p_1 \Vert p_2) +
\tfrac{1}{2}\KL(p_2 \Vert p_1)
\Big].
\end{align}
The training objective is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{\method}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}$ is the base on-policy objective. The implementation keeps the base MLP policy unchanged at inference time; the only overhead is the additional training-time forward passes required for the consistency term.

We also implement teammate dropout as an ablation that zeros a random subset of teammate blocks during training. It is not part of the core method and, empirically, it does not strengthen the story.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Environments.}
The main evidence comes from two PettingZoo MPE tasks \citep{terry2021pettingzoo}: \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, a fully cooperative three-agent coverage task, and \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, where a cooperative team interacts with an adversary. Matrix coordination and stag-hunt variants are retained as fast sanity checks and are reported in the appendix rather than driving the main claim.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare four methods under matched training budgets: IPPO, IPPO+\method, IPPO with a Deep Sets encoder, and Other-Play-style teammate-order randomization \citep{schulman2017ppo, terry2020ps, zaheer2017deepsets, hu2020otherplay}. The comparison is intentionally practical: Deep Sets provides an architectural invariance baseline, and Other-Play provides a symmetry-randomization baseline with a stronger ZSC pedigree.

\paragraph{Main cohort and pilot ablations.}
The main MPE cohort uses 5 seeds, 50k environment steps per seed, and held-out partner evaluation from independently trained policies sharing the same run tag. A 20k, 3-seed pilot sweep selected $\lambda \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.10\}$ for \method\ and evaluated teammate dropout at probability $0.1$. The main 50k PICR-MA setting uses $\lambda=0.02$ because it gave the strongest held-out cross-play among the pilot settings, even though larger $\lambda$ values reduced $\JS$ more aggressively.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
For each run we record training return, held-out partner cross-play return, permutation sensitivity measured by Jensen--Shannon divergence under teammate-order swaps, and environment-specific diagnostics such as collisions or goal-margin statistics. The headline cross-play number in the main table is same-family held-out cross-play, meaning the ego policy is evaluated against independently trained partners from the same method family and run tag; cross-family partners are reported separately in the appendix. Cross-play results are produced from saved checkpoints with a partner-pool filter that excludes the evaluated run itself and respects the intended run tag. To test whether the original matched-seed pattern was stable, we also train a second 5-seed IPPO-versus-\method\ cohort at the same 50k MPE budget and analyze it separately rather than merging it into the baseline-comparison table.

\paragraph{Implementation and compute.}
All experiments were executed sequentially on a single Apple Silicon laptop under the repo's lean-safe resource profile. The current learner is a small NumPy actor--critic rather than a full PyTorch PPO stack; this keeps the artifact compact and reproducible, but it also limits external ambition and informs the workshop-scale framing of the paper. IPPO, \method, and Other-Play share the same MLP backbone and optimization budget, so \method's extra cost comes from two additional training-time forward passes rather than a larger inference model; only Deep Sets changes capacity (1606 versus 3302 cooperative parameters on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, and 1350 versus 3238 on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}).

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Permutation consistency is real.}
Table~\ref{tab:main-results} and Figure~\ref{fig:perm} show that \method\ does what it is supposed to do mechanically. In the main 50k cohort, it lowers teammate-order sensitivity relative to IPPO on both MPE tasks, from $0.0263 \pm 0.0063$ to $0.0127 \pm 0.0019$ on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} and from $0.0102 \pm 0.0066$ to $0.0053 \pm 0.0025$ on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}. Deep Sets drives this diagnostic to zero, as expected for an architectural invariance baseline.

\paragraph{Lower sensitivity does not reliably imply better coordination.}
Figure~\ref{fig:zsc-mismatch} overlays the original matched IPPO$\rightarrow$\method\ seed pairs with the second 5-seed replication cohort, while Table~\ref{tab:replication} gives the corresponding bootstrap summaries. In the original cohort, \method\ lowers $\JS$ in all five \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} pairs and loses on same-family held-out cross-play in all five; the mean cross-play delta is $-1.310$ with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $[-2.145, -0.779]$ after the higher-episode reevaluation. In the replication cohort, \method\ again lowers $\JS$ on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, but now improves same-family held-out cross-play in all five pairs, with mean delta $+3.011$ and interval $[2.603, 3.467]$. Pooling the two cohorts gives a 10-pair \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} cross-play delta of $+0.851$ with interval $[-0.582, 2.233]$, while the pooled $\Delta$JS remains clearly negative at $-0.014$ with interval $[-0.023, -0.006]$. On \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, both cohorts remain positive on average for same-family held-out cross-play ($+3.163$ and $+2.447$), but both intervals remain wide and the pooled 10-pair cross-play interval still spans zero ($[-2.929, 8.842]$). The most reproducible signal is therefore the diagnostic shift, not the coordination gain.

\paragraph{Baselines matter.}
The baseline story is also mixed. Deep Sets is the strongest invariance baseline but is weak on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} held-out cross-play, suggesting that exact invariance alone can remove useful asymmetries in that environment. Other-Play is slightly stronger than \method\ on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} and stronger than the original \method\ cohort on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}. The appendix cross-family breakdown also shows that \method's positive adversary signal is concentrated in same-family partners rather than a uniform gain across partner families. The honest takeaway is therefore not that \method\ is a better default than architectural invariance or role randomization, but that it occupies a small simplicity--performance niche whose coordination effect is too unstable to summarize with a single environment-level sign.

\paragraph{Ablations and overhead.}
The 20k pilot ablations in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} show that stronger regularization does not monotonically help. On \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, increasing $\lambda$ reduces $\JS$ but steadily worsens held-out cross-play. On \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, the mild setting $\lambda=0.02$ gives the best held-out cross-play in the sweep. Teammate dropout harms both tasks. Runtime overhead is moderate rather than negligible: in the main 50k cohort, \method\ costs $1.62\times$ IPPO on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} and $1.33\times$ IPPO on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, while remaining well below the Deep Sets cost on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}.

\section{Discussion}
This paper should not be read as evidence that policy-level permutation consistency is a new default recipe for ZSC in MARL. The stronger and more interesting conclusion is narrower: it is possible to reduce order sensitivity substantially with a very small policy-side intervention, but the downstream value of that intervention is less stable than the diagnostic change it induces.

The contrast between the two MPE tasks is now suggestive rather than decisive. In the original cohort, \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} looked clearly negative and \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} modestly positive; the second matched cohort preserves the adversary-average positivity but flips the spread sign while keeping the diagnostic shift intact. That makes the deeper lesson sharper. Invariance diagnostics measure one property of the policy, and that property can replicate even when the downstream coordination effect remains fragile to the particular seed cohort and partner pool. Lower teammate-order sensitivity is therefore informative, but it is not a reliable proxy for zero-shot coordination quality.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The learner is a compact NumPy implementation rather than a standard PyTorch PPO codebase. The main MPE cohort is stronger than the original smoke runs, but it remains a 50k-step laptop-scale study rather than a large benchmark campaign. Matrix games are useful sanity checks, not headline evidence. These limitations are acceptable for a focused workshop submission, but they should prevent stronger claims than the ones made here.

\section{Conclusion}
\method\ is a clean way to inject permutation consistency into decentralized MARL without changing the inference-time policy. In the current controlled study, that bias reliably lowers teammate-order sensitivity across two matched MPE cohorts, but the paired-seed coordination effect remains unstable: it stays wide on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} and even flips sign across \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} cohorts. For workshop purposes, the more durable contribution is the empirical lesson: lower permutation sensitivity is reproducible here, but it is insufficient as a standalone proxy for zero-shot coordination quality.

\section{Observation Slicing, Hyperparameters, and Assets}
\paragraph{Observation slicing.}
Teammate-block indices are explicitly cached per environment rather than inferred implicitly at each update. For \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, the consistency loss is applied only to the cooperative team. The precise slicing assumptions and any environment-specific simplifications are recorded in \texttt{results/OVERRIDES.md}.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
The main reported learner uses hidden size 64, learning rate $3\times 10^{-3}$, discount 0.99, GAE parameter 0.95, PPO clip ratio 0.2, and four update epochs per rollout. The main PICR-MA cohort uses $\lambda=0.02$ and no teammate dropout. Exact commands, profiles, and artifact inventories are listed in \texttt{results/FINAL\_REPORT.md}.

\paragraph{Model capacity and fairness.}
IPPO, PICR-MA, and Other-Play share the same inference-time MLP and optimizer settings; only Deep Sets changes the encoder capacity. In the main cohort this corresponds to 1606 cooperative parameters for IPPO/PICR-MA/Other-Play versus 3302 for Deep Sets on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, and 1350 versus 3238 on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}. PICR-MA's extra runtime therefore comes from training-time consistency passes rather than a larger deployed policy.

\paragraph{Assets and licenses.}
The experiments rely on PettingZoo 1.24.3, Gymnasium 1.2.3, and SuperSuit 3.10.0. Local package metadata reports MIT licenses for these core environment assets. All reported figures and tables are generated from files stored under \texttt{results/}.
