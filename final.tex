\begin{abstract}
Teammate features in decentralized multi-agent reinforcement learning are often concatenated in an arbitrary fixed order, even when teammates are exchangeable. We study whether reducing this order sensitivity improves zero-shot coordination with unseen partners. Our testbed is \emph{PICR-MA}, a lightweight policy-side regularizer that samples two independent permutations of teammate feature blocks and penalizes the symmetric Kullback--Leibler divergence between the resulting action distributions. \method\ leaves the inference-time policy architecture unchanged and adds only extra training-time forward passes. We evaluate \method\ against IPPO, Deep Sets, and Other-Play on PettingZoo MPE (\texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}) and small matrix-game sanity checks using held-out partner cross-play, permutation sensitivity, and runtime diagnostics. In a 5-seed, 50k-step MPE cohort plus a 20k pilot sweep, \method\ consistently lowers permutation sensitivity and improves held-out-partner coordination over IPPO on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, but it underperforms IPPO, Deep Sets, and Other-Play on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}. Across runs, lower permutation sensitivity is not a reliable proxy for better coordination. The main contribution of the paper is therefore not a new default MARL recipe, but a controlled empirical result: policy-level permutation consistency is a useful bias in some cooperative settings, yet invariance diagnostics alone do not predict zero-shot coordination quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Zero-shot coordination (ZSC) asks whether an agent trained in self-play can cooperate with an unfamiliar partner at test time \citep{hu2020otherplay}. In decentralized MARL, one source of ZSC brittleness is mundane but pervasive: teammate features are commonly fed to a multilayer perceptron in a fixed concatenation order, so the policy may learn distinct roles for the ``first'' and ``second'' teammate even when the environment is symmetric. This creates an avoidable dependence on an arbitrary observation convention.

This paper studies a minimal intervention at the policy level. Rather than changing the policy architecture to a set encoder or graph network \citep{zaheer2017deepsets, liu2020pic}, we regularize the policy to produce similar action distributions under different teammate-order views of the same observation. The resulting method, \method, is intentionally simple: during training we apply two independent permutations to teammate feature blocks and penalize their action-distribution disagreement with a symmetric KL loss.

The interesting question is not only whether this penalty reduces a permutation-sensitivity diagnostic. The more important question is whether that diagnostic reduction improves downstream coordination with held-out partners. The answer in the current artifact is mixed. On \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, \method\ improves held-out-partner coordination over IPPO while also lowering permutation sensitivity. On \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, it lowers permutation sensitivity but still loses to IPPO, Deep Sets, and Other-Play. This mismatch is the main scientific point of the workshop paper.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em]
\item We implement \method\ as a policy-side permutation-consistency regularizer for shared-parameter decentralized MARL with no inference-time architectural change.
\item We build a controlled evaluation stack with held-out partner cross-play, explicit teammate-block slicing, permutation-sensitivity diagnostics, and runtime tracking on MPE and matrix-game sanity checks.
\item We show that \method\ reliably reduces permutation sensitivity in the main MPE cohort, but its effect on held-out coordination is environment-dependent: helpful on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} and harmful on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}.
\item We identify a concrete diagnostic failure mode for this family of ideas: lower teammate-order sensitivity does not by itself imply better zero-shot coordination.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
Each agent observation is decomposed into non-teammate features $s$ and teammate-specific blocks $U = [u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}]$. For two independently sampled teammate permutations $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, we form
\begin{align}
o_{(1)} &= [s \Vert \pi_1(U)], &
p_1 &= \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{(1)}), \\
o_{(2)} &= [s \Vert \pi_2(U)], &
p_2 &= \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{(2)}).
\end{align}
The regularizer is
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{\method}} =
\lambda \, \E \Big[
\tfrac{1}{2}\KL(p_1 \Vert p_2) +
\tfrac{1}{2}\KL(p_2 \Vert p_1)
\Big].
\end{align}
The training objective is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{\method}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}$ is the base on-policy objective. The implementation keeps the base MLP policy unchanged at inference time; the only overhead is the additional training-time forward passes required for the consistency term.

We also implement teammate dropout as an ablation that zeros a random subset of teammate blocks during training. It is not part of the core method and, empirically, it does not strengthen the story.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Environments.}
The main evidence comes from two PettingZoo MPE tasks \citep{terry2021pettingzoo}: \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, a fully cooperative three-agent coverage task, and \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, where a cooperative team interacts with an adversary. Matrix coordination and stag-hunt variants are retained as fast sanity checks and are reported in the appendix rather than driving the main claim.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare four methods under matched training budgets: IPPO, IPPO+\method, IPPO with a Deep Sets encoder, and Other-Play-style teammate-order randomization \citep{schulman2017ppo, terry2020ps, zaheer2017deepsets, hu2020otherplay}. The comparison is intentionally practical: Deep Sets provides an architectural invariance baseline, and Other-Play provides a symmetry-randomization baseline with a stronger ZSC pedigree.

\paragraph{Main cohort and pilot ablations.}
The main MPE cohort uses 5 seeds, 50k environment steps per seed, and held-out partner evaluation from independently trained policies sharing the same run tag. A 20k, 3-seed pilot sweep selected $\lambda \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.10\}$ for \method\ and evaluated teammate dropout at probability $0.1$. The main 50k PICR-MA setting uses $\lambda=0.02$ because it gave the strongest held-out cross-play among the pilot settings, even though larger $\lambda$ values reduced $\JS$ more aggressively.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
For each run we record training return, held-out partner cross-play return, permutation sensitivity measured by Jensen--Shannon divergence under teammate-order swaps, and environment-specific diagnostics such as collisions or goal-margin statistics. Cross-play results are produced from saved checkpoints with a partner-pool filter that excludes the evaluated run itself and respects the intended run tag.

\paragraph{Implementation and compute.}
All experiments were executed sequentially on a single Apple Silicon laptop under the repo's lean-safe resource profile. The current learner is a small NumPy actor--critic rather than a full PyTorch PPO stack; this keeps the artifact compact and reproducible, but it also limits external ambition and informs the workshop-scale framing of the paper.

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Permutation consistency is real.}
Table~\ref{tab:main-results} and Figure~\ref{fig:perm} show that \method\ does what it is supposed to do mechanically. In the main 50k cohort, it lowers teammate-order sensitivity relative to IPPO on both MPE tasks, from $0.0263 \pm 0.0063$ to $0.0127 \pm 0.0019$ on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} and from $0.0102 \pm 0.0066$ to $0.0053 \pm 0.0025$ on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}. Deep Sets drives this diagnostic to zero, as expected for an architectural invariance baseline.

\paragraph{Lower sensitivity does not reliably imply better coordination.}
Figure~\ref{fig:zsc-mismatch} is the core workshop result. On \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, \method\ improves same-family held-out cross-play over IPPO (20.159 $\pm$ 2.043 versus 17.429 $\pm$ 3.770) while reducing $\JS$. On \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, however, \method\ lowers $\JS$ but still achieves the weakest held-out cross-play among the four methods ($-27.206 \pm 0.730$ versus $-25.649 \pm 0.271$ for IPPO, $-25.550 \pm 0.489$ for Deep Sets, and $-25.533 \pm 0.566$ for Other-Play). Across seed-level runs, the correlation between $\JS$ and held-out cross-play is weakly positive on both MPE tasks ($r=0.11$ on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, $r=0.14$ on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}), which directly contradicts a strong ``lower $\JS$ means better ZSC'' story.

\paragraph{Baselines matter.}
The baseline story is also mixed. Deep Sets is the strongest invariance baseline but is weak on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} held-out cross-play, suggesting that exact invariance alone can remove useful asymmetries in that environment. Other-Play is slightly stronger than \method\ on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3} and clearly stronger on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}. The honest takeaway is therefore not that \method\ is a better default than architectural invariance or role randomization, but that it occupies a small simplicity--performance niche: it is lighter than Deep Sets on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, competitive with Other-Play on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, and simple to bolt onto an existing MLP policy.

\paragraph{Ablations and overhead.}
The 20k pilot ablations in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} show that stronger regularization does not monotonically help. On \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, increasing $\lambda$ reduces $\JS$ but steadily worsens held-out cross-play. On \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, the mild setting $\lambda=0.02$ gives the best held-out cross-play in the sweep. Teammate dropout harms both tasks. Runtime overhead is moderate rather than negligible: in the main 50k cohort, \method\ costs $1.62\times$ IPPO on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3} and $1.33\times$ IPPO on \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, while remaining well below the Deep Sets cost on \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}.

\section{Discussion}
This paper should not be read as evidence that policy-level permutation consistency is a new default recipe for ZSC in MARL. The stronger and more interesting conclusion is narrower: it is possible to reduce order sensitivity substantially with a very small policy-side intervention, but the downstream value of that intervention depends strongly on the environment and the competing inductive biases already present in the baseline.

The contrast between the two MPE tasks suggests a practical hypothesis. In \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, reducing brittle teammate-order dependence appears helpful because the cooperative team benefits from a mild symmetry bias without requiring exact exchangeability. In \texttt{simple\_spread\_v3}, the same bias does not rescue coordination and may suppress distinctions that matter for coverage conventions. This interpretation is consistent with the mismatch plot: invariance diagnostics measure one property of the policy, but they do not summarize the entire coordination problem.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The learner is a compact NumPy implementation rather than a standard PyTorch PPO codebase. The main MPE cohort is stronger than the original smoke runs, but it remains a 50k-step laptop-scale study rather than a large benchmark campaign. Matrix games are useful sanity checks, not headline evidence. These limitations are acceptable for a focused workshop submission, but they should prevent stronger claims than the ones made here.

\section{Conclusion}
\method\ is a clean way to inject permutation consistency into decentralized MARL without changing the inference-time policy. In the current controlled study, that bias reliably lowers teammate-order sensitivity and helps held-out coordination in one MPE setting, but it does not transfer uniformly across environments or beat stronger baselines across the board. For workshop purposes, the more durable contribution is the empirical lesson: lower permutation sensitivity is informative but insufficient as a standalone proxy for zero-shot coordination quality.

\section{Observation Slicing, Hyperparameters, and Assets}
\paragraph{Observation slicing.}
Teammate-block indices are explicitly cached per environment rather than inferred implicitly at each update. For \texttt{simple\_adversary\_v3}, the consistency loss is applied only to the cooperative team. The precise slicing assumptions and any environment-specific simplifications are recorded in \texttt{results/OVERRIDES.md}.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
The main reported learner uses hidden size 64, learning rate $3\times 10^{-3}$, discount 0.99, GAE parameter 0.95, PPO clip ratio 0.2, and four update epochs per rollout. The main PICR-MA cohort uses $\lambda=0.02$ and no teammate dropout. Exact commands, profiles, and artifact inventories are listed in \texttt{results/FINAL\_REPORT.md}.

\paragraph{Assets and licenses.}
The experiments rely on PettingZoo 1.24.3, Gymnasium 1.2.3, and SuperSuit 3.10.0. Local package metadata reports MIT licenses for these core environment assets. All reported figures and tables are generated from files stored under \texttt{results/}.
